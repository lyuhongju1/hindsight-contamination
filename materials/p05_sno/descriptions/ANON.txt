An underground neutrino detector sits about 2 km below the surface (about 6,000 m water equivalent) in an active mine.
Its target is 1,000 tonnes of heavy water held in a transparent acrylic sphere of 12 m diameter with a 5.5 cm wall. The sphere hangs from ropes attached to a deck above the cavity.
About 9,500 8-inch photomultiplier tubes, mounted on a 17.8 m diameter geodesic structure, view the sphere. The cavity is filled with about 7,000 tonnes of ultrapure light water, which shields the target and surrounds both the sphere and the tubes.
The detector records Cherenkov light from 8B solar neutrinos through charged-current, neutral-current and elastic-scattering reactions on deuterium and electrons. Its analysis threshold is a few MeV.
With the heavy-water programme finished, the collaboration plans a follow-on programme that reuses the cavity, the acrylic sphere, the photomultiplier array and the light-water shield.
The follow-on programme targets solar neutrinos well below 1 MeV (pep and CNO), geoneutrinos and reactor antineutrinos, and a search for neutrinoless double-beta decay.
These goals require visible energies down to a few hundred keV, with an energy resolution of a few percent near 1 MeV.
The heavy water is returned after the heavy-water programme. The acrylic sphere, photomultiplier array and light-water shield are retained.
