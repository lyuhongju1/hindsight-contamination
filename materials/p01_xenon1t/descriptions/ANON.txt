A dual-phase xenon time projection chamber operates in a deep underground laboratory to search for weakly interacting massive particles scattering on xenon nuclei.
The cryostat holds about 3.2 t of xenon, of which about 2.0 t lies inside the active volume of a cylindrical chamber roughly 1 m in diameter and 1 m tall, viewed by about 250 three-inch photomultipliers in top and bottom arrays.
A particle interaction produces a prompt scintillation signal in the liquid and free electrons that drift upward to the liquid surface, where they are extracted into the gas and produce a delayed proportional signal; the time between the two gives the depth.
At the operating drift field the longest drift time is close to 0.7 ms, so electronegative impurities in the xenon directly reduce the charge signal from deep events.
The xenon is continuously extracted from the cryostat, evaporated, passed through heated zirconium getters in two parallel loops, and recondensed; a cryogenic distillation column removes krypton.
The detector sits in a water tank instrumented as a Cherenkov muon veto.
The electron lifetime must be at least as long as the maximum drift time.
The background from internal radioactive contaminants must stay below that from detector materials in the inner fiducial mass of about 1.3 t.
The instrument must reach a spin-independent cross-section sensitivity near 1.6e-47 cm^2 for a 50 GeV/c^2 particle with an exposure of about 2 t y.
