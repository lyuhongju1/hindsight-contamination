Advanced LIGO, as operated in its first observing run (2015), consists of two detectors, at Hanford and Livingston, each a dual-recycled Michelson interferometer with 4 km Fabry-Perot arm cavities.
A 1064 nm Nd:YAG laser delivers roughly 20 W to the interferometer; power recycling raises the light on the beamsplitter to several kilowatts and the arm cavities store about 100 kW.
The 40 kg fused-silica test masses hang from quadruple pendulum suspensions on actively isolated in-vacuum platforms.
The gravitational-wave signal leaves the antisymmetric port, passes a suspended output mode cleaner on an isolated in-vacuum platform, and is detected on in-vacuum photodiodes.
The signal is read with DC readout: a differential arm offset of about 10 pm places tens of milliwatts of carrier at the antisymmetric port, which serves as the reference field.
The physics goal is direct detection of gravitational waves from compact-binary coalescences between about 10 Hz and a few kHz.
The strain sensitivity reaches about 1e-23 per root hertz near 100 Hz.
The detectors must reach quantum-noise-limited sensitivity above roughly 50 Hz, with every technical noise at least a factor of ten below the quantum noise across the band.
